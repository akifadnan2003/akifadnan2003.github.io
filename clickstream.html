Clickstream Analytics Dashboard

What visitors do on an online store, from the first page view to the order

Clickstream BigQuery SQL Next.js TypeScript Cloud Run
Back to Projects

Overview

An online store records every click: pages viewed, searches, filters, menu and banner clicks, products added to the basket. On their own these events are just noise. This dashboard turns them into answers the product, sales and marketing teams can act on: where visitors drop off, what they search for and do not find, and which products are looked at but not bought.

I built it together with a teammate. My part was the data pipeline that keeps the reporting tables fresh, single sign-on, deployment, the Turkish and English interface, and checking that the numbers on screen match the sales records.

What it shows

Daily funnel

How many visitors move from a product page to the basket, the checkout and the order, day by day, so a drop shows up the morning after it happens.

Searches that find nothing

The search terms that return no results. Each one is a product people wanted and could not find on the site.

Filters that lead nowhere

Filter combinations that end on an empty page, split by brand, so the catalogue and the filters can be fixed.

Viewed but not bought

Products with many views and few orders, a starting point for checking price, photos or stock.

Recommendations and cross-sell

How often suggested products are clicked and bought, so it is clear whether the suggestions earn their place on the page.

Business buyers hiding as guests

Visitors who browse and order like trade customers but are not registered as one, a list the sales team can follow up.

How it works

Site events (clickstream) + sales records └── Reporting tables in BigQuery, rebuilt every night by scheduled SQL Web app (Next.js, TypeScript, Cloud Run) ├── Reads only the reporting tables, never the raw events ├── Date, store and country filters on every page ├── Turkish / English interface └── Single sign-on through the company login

What I learnt

An empty chart is usually a data problem, not a chart problem. When the panel went blank for a few days, the events were still there; the nightly job that builds the reporting tables had stopped on one failing step. Fixing that job mattered more than any new feature.

Before a number goes on a dashboard, write down exactly how it is counted, including repeat clicks and bots. That conversation with the teams who will use it is half the work.
